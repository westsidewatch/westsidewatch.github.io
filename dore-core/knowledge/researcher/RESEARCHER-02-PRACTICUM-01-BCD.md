## B — Geography / chronology transfer

### Research problem
When ONE presents a biblical route/place/chronology claim, how should Doré decide what may be asserted rather than merely reproduce a map or editorial route?

### Uncertainty map
A biblical narrative can explicitly name places while leaving the exact path, intermediate stops, date, distance, or modern identification uncertain. A map is a reconstruction, not the event itself.

### Evidence plan
1. Extract explicit place and sequence anchors from the biblical text.
2. Separate textual anchors from ONE editorial route data.
3. Inspect provenance of any external map/atlas/source.
4. Distinguish ancient textual identification, modern site identification, and reconstructed route.
5. Refuse precision not supported by the evidence.

### Competing hypotheses
H1: a displayed route is directly established by Scripture.
H2: Scripture establishes endpoints/order but the drawn route is a scholarly/editorial reconstruction.
H3: even one or more site identifications are disputed, making route precision weaker still.

### Falsification reflex
A route claim fails if the text does not state the intermediate path, the external source lacks provenance, or competing site identifications materially alter the route.

### Bounded conclusion
Doré may state explicit biblical place/sequence anchors with high confidence when verified. It must label reconstructed paths, dates, distances, and site identifications according to their evidential status. ONE map geometry cannot become Core evidence by repetition.

### Autonomous next action
When geography matters, retrieve the primary passage first, then evaluate specialist map/archaeological/geographical sources independently and record provenance.

### Consolidation
CANDIDATE_FOR_EXAM — method only; no particular reconstructed route promoted to durable fact.

## C — Intertext transfer

### Research problem
When ONE links two passages, how should Doré decide whether the relationship is quotation, allusion, thematic parallel, or editorial association?

### Evidence plan
1. Read both passages in context.
2. Check explicit quotation formula where relevant.
3. Compare distinctive shared wording and syntax in relevant original-language/textual witnesses.
4. Test whether shared language is common/formulaic rather than distinctive.
5. Ask whether understanding the proposed source context materially explains the receptor text.
6. Where dependence is claimed, evaluate direction rather than assuming chronology alone proves literary borrowing.

### External methodological check
Academic literature confirms that biblical intertextuality requires criteria rather than mere resemblance. Work on inner-biblical quotation stresses distinctive syntactic/verbal parallels, contextual dependence, and signs of interpretive reuse; methodological surveys also note continuing disagreement over how to distinguish legitimate intertexts from coincidental similarity.

### Classification ladder
- EXPLICIT_QUOTATION — marked or otherwise strongly demonstrable quotation.
- PROBABLE_ALLUSION — distinctive cumulative evidence of intentional reference.
- THEMATIC_PARALLEL — meaningful conceptual similarity without adequate evidence of literary dependence.
- EDITORIAL_ASSOCIATION — useful study linkage, but not demonstrated textual dependence.

### Falsification reflex
Many weak parallels do not become a strong allusion merely by accumulation. Common vocabulary, stock phrases, or reader association can defeat a claimed dependency.

### Bounded conclusion
A ONE cross-reference is a research lead. Doré must classify the relationship from evidence; it cannot inherit ONE's implied category automatically.

### Autonomous next action
For consequential intertext claims, inspect original wording/context and consult methodological scholarship before consolidation.

### Consolidation
CANDIDATE_FOR_EXAM — classification method, not individual ONE links.

## D — Blind unseen question

### Question selected without supplied decomposition template
`保羅說「聖經都是神所默示的」時，「聖經」是否包括後來的新約？`

### Initial uncertainty map
The question mixes lexical meaning, historical referent, canonical development, and later theological application. A later completed canon cannot simply be assumed as the immediate historical referent of an earlier sentence.

### Doré-generated decomposition
- What expression is used for “Scripture” in 2 Tim 3:15–16?
- What writings were Timothy said to know from childhood?
- What body of sacred writings would be historically available in that setting?
- Do any NT texts call other emerging Christian writings “Scripture,” and what would that establish?
- What is immediate referent versus later canonical/theological extension?
- Does a doctrine about the final canon answer the historical-semantic question automatically?

### Evidence requirements
Primary Greek text/context; lexical/grammatical evidence; historically responsible work on early Christian/Jewish Scripture; evidence for early intra-Christian use of γραφή terminology; canonical-history scholarship; competing interpretations.

### Competing hypotheses
H1: 2 Tim 3:16 immediately refers primarily to the Jewish sacred writings/OT known to Timothy.
H2: the wording intentionally includes some contemporary apostolic Christian writings as Scripture.
H3: the immediate historical referent is earlier sacred writings, while later Christian canonical theology legitimately applies the proposition to the completed canon; this application must be distinguished from immediate referent.

### Falsification targets
Evidence that the local context explicitly identifies a broader corpus; evidence that γραφή here demonstrably functions as an open technical category including specified Christian texts; or evidence undermining the assumed historical setting.

### Bounded preliminary conclusion
The immediate contextual question cannot be answered merely by importing the later 27-book NT canon. The childhood-learning context strongly requires investigation of Jewish sacred writings as the immediate referent. Whether and how the statement extends to emerging or later canonical Christian writings is a separate canonical/theological research question. No durable doctrinal conclusion is consolidated from this practicum alone.

### Autonomous next action
Run a dedicated lexical/contextual study of 2 Tim 3:14–17 and a separate canonical-history study of NT uses of γραφή for other Christian writings, then compare without collapsing the two questions.
