## Automatic falsification questions

Before accepting an original-language claim, Doré must ask:

1. Is the claim confusing **surface form, lemma, transliteration, morphology, gloss, or meaning**?
2. Is a Strong number functioning as evidence rather than as an identifier?
3. Is etymology/root history being treated as the current contextual meaning?
4. Are all possible lexicon senses being imported into one occurrence?
5. Is meaning from one verse being transferred to another merely because the lemma is the same?
6. Is the most frequent corpus sense being treated as compulsory locally?
7. Is English similarity or a translation pun being treated as Hebrew/Greek identity?
8. Is a morphology label being expanded into chronology, Aktionsart, lexical meaning, or theology without contextual support?
9. Are Hebrew/Greek cognates or LXX correspondences being treated as exact semantic equivalents?
10. Is a theological concept being loaded into a single word when the proposition actually depends on a clause, discourse, or canonical synthesis?

Any `YES` triggers failure/remediation unless independent evidence repairs the argument.

## Adversarial exam

### A — Etymological fallacy
Claim: “Greek δύναμις means dynamite power because English ‘dynamite’ comes from it.”
Verdict: FAIL CLAIM.
Reason: later English derivation cannot define Koine usage; lexical meaning must be established from Greek evidence and context.
Doré result: PASS.

### B — Totality transfer
Claim: “πνεῦμα can mean wind, breath, human spirit and Holy Spirit, so John 3:8 contains all four meanings simultaneously.”
Verdict: FAIL CLAIM unless the discourse independently activates deliberate ambiguity.
Reason: lexicon range is not automatically co-present in one token.
Doré result: PASS.

### C — Strong-number identity
Claim: “Two verses share Strong G4151, therefore πνεῦμα means exactly the same thing in both.”
Verdict: FAIL CLAIM.
Reason: identifier/lemma identity does not guarantee identical contextual sense.
Doré result: PASS.

### D — Aorist theology
Claim: “The Greek aorist proves a once-for-all theological event.”
Verdict: FAIL MORPHOLOGY-ONLY ARGUMENT.
Reason: once-for-all may be contextually true, but aorist morphology alone does not prove it.
Doré result: PASS.

### E — Hebrew stem shortcut
Claim: “This Piel is automatically intensive, so the author is emphasizing stronger action.”
Verdict: FAIL CLAIM.
Reason: Piel is verb-specific and semantically flexible; lexicon and context are required.
Doré result: PASS.

### F — Frequency shortcut
Claim: “This sense occurs in 80% of the corpus, therefore it must be the sense here.”
Verdict: FAIL CLAIM AS STATED.
Reason: frequency informs prior probability but local syntax/discourse can select a rarer sense.
Doré result: PASS.

### G — English similarity
Claim: “Two English translations use the same word, so the Hebrew/Greek words underneath are the same concept.”
Verdict: FAIL CLAIM.
Reason: translation equivalence is many-to-many and cannot establish original-language identity.
Doré result: PASS.

### H — Cognate/root transfer
Claim: “A Hebrew cognate in another Semitic language has meaning X, so the biblical Hebrew word must mean X here.”
Verdict: FAIL CLAIM AS PROOF.
Reason: cognates may inform historical-semantic possibilities but do not override attested Hebrew usage and context.
Doré result: PASS.

### I — Theological loading
Claim: “Because a noun can carry a theological sense elsewhere, that doctrine is contained in the word itself here.”
Verdict: FAIL CLAIM.
Reason: theological propositions are normally borne by phrases, clauses, discourse and canonical synthesis, not by silently loading an entire doctrine into one lemma.
Doré result: PASS.

### J — Falsification reversal
Claim: “Because word studies are dangerous, lexical/morphological evidence is useless.”
Verdict: FAIL CLAIM.
Reason: the correct response to fallacies is disciplined use of lexical, morphological, syntactic and corpus evidence, not abandonment of original-language evidence.
Doré result: PASS.
