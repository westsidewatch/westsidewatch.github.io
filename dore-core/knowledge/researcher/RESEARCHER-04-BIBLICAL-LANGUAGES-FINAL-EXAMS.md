## Exam B — Unseen Hebrew transfer: נֶפֶשׁ in Genesis 2:7

### Blind claim
“Genesis 2:7 says God inserted an immortal soul (`nephesh`) into Adam, because nephesh means soul.”

### Analysis

1. **Text/form**: the clause says the human `became` (`וַיְהִי`) `לְנֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה`, a living `nephesh`/living being.
2. **Lexical range**: Hebrew `נֶפֶשׁ` has usages including living being/person, life/self, desire and related senses; one English gloss “soul” does not exhaust or define it.
3. **Syntax/context**: the immediate creation sentence describes the human becoming a living being after receiving the breath of life. The wording is not simply “God placed a nephesh inside the man.”
4. **Counter-check**: scholarly Hebrew discussion explicitly notes that Gen 2:7 describes what the human becomes; the verse alone does not lexicalize a later philosophical doctrine of an independently immortal soul.
5. **Theological boundary**: doctrines of postmortem existence or soul/body anthropology may draw on wider biblical/canonical evidence, but they cannot be proven from `נֶפֶשׁ = immortal soul` as a dictionary equation.

Verdict: **CLAIM FAILS AS A LEXICAL ARGUMENT**. The verse can participate in theological anthropology, but not by the proposed gloss shortcut.
Result: PASS.

Evidence consulted:
- SBL open-access Richard C. Steiner, *Disembodied Souls*, on Gen 2:7 and `נפש חיה`;
- SBL Hebrew teaching material noting “living being” as the more accurate local rendering;
- comparative Hebrew lexical/context discussion.

## Exam C — Unseen Greek transfer: ἀγαπάω / φιλέω in John 21:15–17

### Blind claim
“Jesus uses `agapaō` and Peter uses `phileō`, proving Jesus asks for divine unconditional love while Peter can offer only inferior friendship-love; the third question lowers Jesus' standard.”

### Analysis

1. **Surface/lemma**: two different Greek lemmas do alternate in the passage. That is real data.
2. **Fallacy check**: different lemmas do not automatically entail a ranked semantic contrast in every context.
3. **Authorial/corpus check**: John can use the two verbs in overlapping ways elsewhere; for example, descriptions of the Father's love for the Son use both verbal families in different contexts.
4. **Discourse check**: John 21 also varies other near-synonymous vocabulary in the same repeated exchange (sheep/lamb/tend/feed patterns), making stylistic variation a serious counter-hypothesis.
5. **Scholarly counter-check**: contemporary commentary and detailed translation notes report that most modern interpreters do not regard the alternation here as proving two ranked kinds of love.
6. **Boundary**: subtle connotative differences between synonyms are not impossible in Greek generally; the stronger claim fails because this context does not establish the theological hierarchy merely from the two dictionary entries.

Verdict: **THE TWO LEMMAS ARE REAL; THE PROPOSED TWO-LEVEL THEOLOGY IS NOT PROVEN BY THEM**.
Result: PASS.

## Exam D — Blind theological/original-language claim: אֱלֹהִים in Genesis 1:1

### Claim
“`Elohim` is morphologically plural, so Genesis 1:1 directly proves the Trinity in Hebrew grammar.”

### Analysis

1. `אֱלֹהִים` has plural morphology and can function as an ordinary plural in some contexts.
2. In Genesis 1:1 its verbal agreement is singular (`בָּרָא`, “he created”), and the discourse treats the referent as the singular creator God.
3. Therefore the morphology is not equivalent to “three persons.” The form itself supplies no grammatical number three and no lexical specification of Father/Son/Spirit.
4. Christian Trinitarian theology may read Genesis canonically alongside later texts, but that is a canonical-theological argument, not a direct morphological proof from the `-im` ending.
5. The inverse claim — that the plural form makes Trinitarian canonical interpretation impossible — also exceeds the grammar.

Verdict: **FAIL AS A DIRECT HEBREW-GRAMMAR PROOF; canonical theology must be labeled separately**.
Result: PASS.
