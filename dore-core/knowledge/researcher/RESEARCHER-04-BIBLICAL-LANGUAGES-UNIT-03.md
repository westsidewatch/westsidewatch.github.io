## Objective

Learn to use Greek case and Hebrew noun state as grammatical evidence without collapsing a form into one fixed English gloss or syntactic function.

## Greek evidence

Ewald's open elementary New Testament Greek course places cases and gender in Chapter 3 and prepositions in Chapter 4, confirming that case belongs to the grammar/syntax layer rather than to lexical meaning alone.

An inspectable open Greek grammar summarizes the four common cases as nominative, genitive, dative and accusative, but explicitly frames their familiar functions as common rather than exhaustive: nominative often marks subject/predicate nominative, genitive often possession/"of", dative often indirect object/"to/for", accusative often direct object. The same source notes that case carries a range of possible information and must be understood in context.

## Hebrew evidence

The unfoldingWord Hebrew Grammar, identified by OSHB as a companion grammar built around its morphology system, distinguishes absolute and construct state. It notes that many masculine singular nouns can look identical in both states, so form alone is not always decisive. A construct form links grammatically to what follows in a construct chain; the final term is absolute and can determine definiteness of the chain. Crucially, the grammar lists multiple relations a construct chain can express: possessive, material, attributive, purpose/result, agent/instrument, subject/object of an action, and others.

Sources:
- https://uhg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/state_absolute.html
- https://uhg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/state_construct.html
- OSHB release note pointing to the companion grammar: https://github.com/openscriptures/morphhb/releases/tag/v2.2

## Learned rules

1. `GRAMMATICAL FORM ≠ SINGLE SYNTACTIC FUNCTION`.
2. Greek case narrows and organizes possible syntactic relations, but does not map one-to-one to an English preposition or role.
3. A nominative is often a subject, an accusative often a direct object, a genitive often expresses an "of" relation, and a dative often an indirect object—but "often" must not be converted into "always".
4. Hebrew construct state marks grammatical dependency inside a chain, not one fixed semantic relation such as possession.
5. Hebrew construct/absolute state may be morphologically ambiguous in some forms; syntax and neighboring words can be necessary to identify the state.
6. Definiteness and relation in a construct chain are properties of the construction, not recoverable from an isolated gloss alone.
7. Translation labels such as "of", "to", "for", possessive apostrophe-s, or direct-object word order are outputs of interpretation/translation, not the morphology itself.

## Self exam

### Q1 — Greek genitive
May Doré translate every genitive with English "of" and call the analysis complete?

Answer: No. "Of" is a frequent pedagogical approximation, but Greek genitive relations are broader and must be determined syntactically/contextually.
Result: PASS.

### Q2 — Greek dative
If a noun is dative, must it be an indirect object?

Answer: No. Indirect object is common, but the dative can support other relations; case is evidence to be interpreted in construction.
Result: PASS.

### Q3 — Hebrew construct
Does construct state prove possession?

Answer: No. Construct chains can express possession among several possible relations, including material, attribution, purpose/result, agent/instrument and subject/object relations.
Result: PASS.

### Q4 — Hebrew form ambiguity
If a masculine singular noun has the same visible form in absolute and construct state, can the state be decided from the isolated spelling alone?

Answer: Not reliably. The grammar explicitly notes identical-looking forms; syntactic context must be inspected.
Result: PASS.

## Transfer exam

Unseen prompt: “This Greek noun is genitive, therefore the English translation must contain ‘of’.”

Required reasoning: reject the one-to-one mapping. Genitive constrains syntactic analysis but does not prescribe one English surface translation.
Result: PASS.

Unseen prompt: “The Hebrew phrase is a construct chain, therefore it means ownership.”

Required reasoning: reject the semantic shortcut. Construct state establishes grammatical linkage; the semantic relation must be inferred from the lexical and discourse context.
Result: PASS.
