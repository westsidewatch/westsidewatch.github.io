## Objective

Learn to use immediate syntax/discourse and corpus comparison to narrow lexical possibilities without committing concordance, frequency, root, or totality-transfer fallacies.

## Evidence base

Open lexical-semantics teaching repeatedly identifies context as the control on local sense and warns against importing a word's whole semantic range into one occurrence. An academic discussion of ambiguity in Luke-Acts likewise notes that context normally closes off many potential senses while allowing genuine ambiguity only when multiple senses remain strongly activated. Benjamin Baxter's published survey of Hebrew/Greek word-study fallacies explicitly treats word-meaning as a contextual problem and provides cross-language examples.

## Learned rules

1. `CONTEXT SELECTS LOCAL SENSE FROM LEXICAL POSSIBILITIES`.
2. The sentence/clause and immediate discourse outrank a decontextualized concordance list when determining the local sense.
3. Corpus comparison is comparative evidence, not a vote. A more frequent sense elsewhere does not mechanically override strong local syntax/discourse.
4. Authorial usage, genre, collocation, syntactic frame and discourse function can make some corpus parallels more relevant than raw frequency.
5. Rare sense ≠ impossible sense. Frequent sense ≠ automatic sense.
6. Similar wording in another verse can support a hypothesis only when the grammatical and discourse environments are sufficiently comparable.
7. Genuine ambiguity must be evidence-led: preserve multiple senses only when context activates more than one; do not invent “deeper” ambiguity from a lexicon list.
8. Concordance work is best used to generate and test hypotheses, not to collect every occurrence and pour all meanings into the target verse.

## Self exam

### Q1
A lemma means X in 70% of corpus hits and Y in 30%. Must the target verse mean X?
Answer: No. Frequency is prior evidence, not a verdict; local syntax/discourse can favor Y.
Result: PASS.

### Q2
Ten verses share the same lemma but occur in different genres and constructions. Are they equally probative parallels?
Answer: No. Grammatical frame, author, genre, collocation and discourse role affect comparability.
Result: PASS.

### Q3
A lexicon lists three senses and the target sentence plausibly supports only one. Should Doré mention all three as active meanings?
Answer: No. That would be illegitimate totality transfer unless the discourse genuinely activates ambiguity.
Result: PASS.

### Q4
Could a rare corpus sense still be correct locally?
Answer: Yes, if form, syntax, collocation and discourse strongly support it; rarity lowers prior expectation but does not prohibit it.
Result: PASS.

## Transfer exam

Unseen prompt: “Most uses of this Greek word mean X, so this verse must mean X too.”
Required reasoning: treat frequency as supporting evidence only; inspect local grammar, collocation, discourse, author/genre parallels and counterexamples before deciding.
Result: PASS.

Unseen prompt: “I found every occurrence in Strong’s and combined all of them into a rich definition.”
Required reasoning: reject the method. A concordance supplies data locations; local senses must be distinguished before synthesis.
Result: PASS.

## Failure traps checked

- `MOST FREQUENT SENSE = LOCAL SENSE`: rejected.
- `CONCORDANCE = DEFINITION`: rejected.
- `ALL PARALLELS EQUALLY RELEVANT`: rejected.
- `RARE = IMPOSSIBLE`: rejected.
- `POSSIBLE AMBIGUITY = INTENDED AMBIGUITY`: rejected.
- `ALL CORPUS SENSES IN ONE VERSE`: rejected.
