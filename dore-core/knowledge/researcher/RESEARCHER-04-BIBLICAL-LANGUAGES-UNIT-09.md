## Practicum A — γραφή

### Lexical evidence

Abbott-Smith gives the general semantic background `writing / that which is written`, then documents New Testament use of `ἡ γραφή / αἱ γραφαί` for sacred writings/Scripture. It also distinguishes common plural/corpus usage from many singular references to a particular scriptural passage. This is a usage pattern, not a rule that can be applied without context.

Inspectable source: Abbott-Smith entry for `γραφή` exposed through StudyLight; cross-check with public Greek lexicon interfaces.

### Morphology and local-context checks

`γραφή` is a feminine noun; case/number mark its local syntactic role but do not themselves identify which books or passage the writer intends. Thus:
- singular `γραφή` does not lexically mean “one Bible verse” by definition;
- plural `γραφαί` does not lexically prove a later fixed 66-book canon;
- referential scope is a discourse/historical question.

John 10:35 has `ἡ γραφή` in the context of a cited line from Psalm 82. The singular noun is compatible with reference to Scripture/the scriptural authority invoked in the argument; the form alone does not settle whether the author conceptualizes a single passage, Scripture as an authority, or both through metonymy.

### 1 Corinthians 15:3–4

Paul's formula says twice that Christ's death/resurrection occurred `κατὰ τὰς γραφάς` — “according to the Scriptures.” In this mid-first-century Pauline context, scholarly discussion identifies the authoritative scriptural matrix as Israel's/Jewish Scriptures, not as a claim that the later New Testament canon already existed in its final form.

Claim ledger:

#### G1 — Did Paul have “Scripture” language in the apostolic era?
Yes. `γραφαί` and related formulas are already normal authoritative-scripture language in Paul's letters.
Confidence: HIGH.

#### G2 — Does 1 Corinthians 15:3–4 prove the complete later Christian Bible already existed?
No. The phrase indicates conformity to authoritative Scriptures available to Paul/early Christians, principally Israel's Scriptures in this context. It does not establish a completed later NT canon.
Decision: REJECT the inference.

#### G3 — Does `κατὰ τὰς γραφάς` identify one exact proof text for death and one exact proof text for resurrection?
Not by grammar alone. The plural formula can invoke the scriptural witness broadly. Specific proposed passages (for example Isaiah/Psalms/Hosea/Jonah traditions) require intertextual argument; the phrase itself does not name them.
Decision: NOT ESTABLISHED by the lexical phrase alone.

#### G4 — Could early Christian writings later be described with `γραφή` language?
Yes; 2 Peter 3:16 compares Paul's letters with `τὰς λοιπὰς γραφάς` (“the other Scriptures/writings”), an important datum for later first-century/early-Christian canon formation. But that later datum must not be read backward as though 1 Corinthians 15 referred to a completed NT collection.
Decision: CONFIRMED as a diachronic distinction.

### Counter-checks

- `γραφή = modern physical Bible`: rejected. The noun denotes writing/Scripture, not a modern bound-book concept.
- `plural Scriptures = final canon list`: rejected. Plural morphology does not define canon boundaries.
- `Paul says Scriptures = Gospels already written and included`: rejected as an unsupported historical leap.
- inverse `NT writings could never be called Scripture`: rejected; 2 Peter 3:16 is counter-evidence.

## Practicum B — unseen Greek transfer

### Transfer 1 — article + noun
Prompt: “A Greek noun has the definite article. Does that automatically make it a proper name?”
Answer: No. The article marks definiteness/discourse status in ways that depend on Greek syntax; proper-name status and referent require lexical/contextual evidence.
Result: PASS.

### Transfer 2 — plural category
Prompt: “A plural noun means a defined collection, so its members must be recoverable from the morphology alone.”
Answer: No. Number tells us plurality; collection boundaries are pragmatic/historical/contextual evidence.
Result: PASS.

### Transfer 3 — lexical range to local sense
Prompt: “Because `γραφή` can mean writing in wider Greek, every NT occurrence might just mean an ordinary document.”
Answer: No. Lexical possibility is constrained by established NT collocations and local discourse. Sacred-Scripture usage is strongly attested, while ordinary-document senses cannot be inserted against context.
Result: PASS.

### Transfer 4 — local sense to canon history
Prompt: “Once a verse clearly means Scripture, the word itself tells us exactly which books were canonical for that author.”
Answer: No. Lexical classification as Scripture and historical reconstruction of canon extent are different claims.
Result: PASS.

## Adversarial gate

Tempting claim: “Paul says ‘according to the Scriptures’ in 1 Corinthians 15, therefore the apostles already possessed the Bible exactly as Christians have it today.”

Falsification:
1. lexical form establishes authoritative scriptural writings, not a modern binding/canon list;
2. Paul's first-century context points to Israel's Scriptures as the inherited scriptural corpus;
3. later Christian writings and collection/canon history must be investigated diachronically;
4. 2 Peter 3:16 shows that the category could later be applied to Pauline letters, which demonstrates development rather than proving a completed NT corpus at Paul's earlier statement.

Verdict: CLAIM FAILS AS STATED.
Doré result: PASS.
