## Practicum A — רוּחַ

### Prior claim under review

Earlier research concluded:
- the Hebrew Bible clearly speaks of God's/YHWH's `ruach` and attributes divine activity to it;
- `ruach` itself is not a technical one-sense label meaning the later Christian doctrinal person "the Holy Spirit";
- full Trinitarian synthesis requires a separate canonical/theological layer.

### Lexical counter-check

BDB organizes `רוּחַ` across a broad range that includes breath, wind, life-related breath/spirit, disposition and various spirit usages. The breadth of the entry directly falsifies `רוּחַ = Holy Spirit in every occurrence` and also falsifies `רוּחַ = one fixed English gloss`.

Evidence surface:
- BDB entry exposed at BibleHub/BLB for H7307, with distinct breath/wind/spirit categories and numerous sub-usages.

### Contextual claim ledger

#### A1 — `רוּחַ` has genuine lexical breadth
Evidence: BDB separates breath, wind and spirit/disposition usages and lists concrete contexts for each.
Decision: CONFIRMED, high confidence.

#### A2 — construct or relational grammar does not by itself settle the semantic relation
A phrase such as `רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים` can grammatically link `ruach` with God, but the construction alone cannot answer every interpretive question about whether a passage emphasizes divine Spirit, wind from God, breath, agency, or another contextually constrained relation. The lexical range and discourse remain necessary.
Decision: CONFIRMED as method boundary.

#### A3 — divine-Spirit language exists independently of a one-word lexical shortcut
Passages that explicitly relate `ruach` to God/YHWH and describe divine activity provide the textual basis. The conclusion does not depend on pretending every `ruach` token means Holy Spirit.
Decision: CONFIRMED; this strengthens the earlier textual conclusion.

#### A4 — Nicene personhood is the lexical meaning of `ruach`
Evidence: none; category error.
Decision: REJECT. Personhood/systematic identification is canonical/theological synthesis, not the dictionary meaning of the noun.

### Counter-pressure retained

- Some famous passages can support more than one lexical translation at the local level (wind/spirit), so an English capitalization choice is not Hebrew morphology.
- Later Christian canonical identification may be legitimate within that framework, but it must be labeled as canonical theology rather than lexical fact.

### Practicum result for product node

The existing `research.ot.divine-spirit` conclusion is materially strengthened on the Hebrew lexical layer: its "yes" answer remains justified when the question means whether the OT speaks about God's Spirit/divine activity, while its theological boundary remains necessary. Status should remain `CANDIDATE_FOR_EXAM`, not be promoted to full consolidation, because canonical-transfer work is still outstanding.

## Practicum B — שָׂטָן

### Prior claim under review

Earlier blind research kept the question `約伯記中的撒但就是新約的魔鬼嗎？` at `WORKING`, because lexical/narrative, diachronic and canonical layers had not been separated with sufficient Hebrew evidence.

### Lexical evidence

BDB distinguishes:
1. ordinary `שָׂטָן` as an adversary/opponent in human or national contexts;
2. the superhuman `הַשָּׂטָן` in Job and Zechariah;
3. unarticulated `שָׂטָן` in 1 Chronicles 21:1, treated by BDB as a proper-name use.

This distribution is methodologically important. The same lemma does not force one identity or role across all occurrences.

### Article/context evidence

Job repeatedly has `הַשָּׂטָן`, "the satan/adversary," in a heavenly-council setting. Zechariah 3 likewise uses `הַשָּׂטָן` in a heavenly judicial/adversarial scene. Scholarly Hebrew-Bible treatments commonly distinguish these articulated role-like uses from the later proper-name use in 1 Chronicles 21:1.

Counter-check: the definite article is significant evidence against simply assuming a proper name in Job, but article/no-article is not by itself a complete history-of-concepts argument. Narrative role, dating, comparative texts and later Jewish/NT evidence remain separate requirements.

### Claim ledger

#### B1 — Hebrew `שָׂטָן` means only the Christian Devil
Evidence against: ordinary adversary uses in Numbers, Samuel, Kings and Psalms; BDB lexical division.
Decision: REJECT.

#### B2 — Job itself presents `הַשָּׂטָן` as a member/participant in the heavenly scene with an adversarial/testing function
Evidence: Job 1–2 narrative and the articulated form; scholarly descriptions of the divine-council scene.
Decision: CONFIRMED at narrative/textual level.

#### B3 — Job's grammar alone proves that this figure cannot be related to the later Satan/Devil
Evidence: insufficient.
Decision: REJECT inverse shortcut. Grammar can distinguish Job's presentation; it cannot by itself forbid a later diachronic/canonical relationship.

#### B4 — Job's `הַשָּׂטָן` is lexically identical to the fully developed New Testament Devil concept
Evidence: insufficient; historical-development and NT comparison are required.
Decision: NOT ESTABLISHED.

#### B5 — Zechariah 3 is a relevant Hebrew-Bible comparison
Evidence: same articulated noun in a heavenly adversarial/judicial context.
Decision: CONFIRMED; comparison strengthens the role/title reading at the Hebrew-Bible layer.

#### B6 — 1 Chronicles 21:1 supplies evidence of a later/different grammatical presentation
Evidence: unarticulated `שָׂטָן`; BDB treats it as proper-name usage; scholarly discussion recognizes a development relative to Job/Zechariah.
Decision: CONFIRMED as comparative datum, not by itself a complete evolutionary theory.

### Scholarly counter-check

Not all specialists agree that "accuser/prosecutor" is the best lexical gloss for every supernatural `satan` passage. Some recent discussion argues the Hebrew term more fundamentally denotes an adversary/attacker and that "accuser" can overstate the lexical evidence. Therefore Doré should prefer the broader wording "the adversary" when discussing the lemma itself and describe courtroom/accusatory function only where the narrative supports it.

### Practicum result for product node

The existing `research.job.satan-identity` node can now give a more useful Hebrew-layer answer:
- Job presents `הַשָּׂטָן`, "the adversary," in its own heavenly-council narrative;
- the lemma also has ordinary adversary uses elsewhere;
- Zechariah 3 is a close articulated comparison;
- 1 Chronicles 21:1 has a different unarticulated presentation;
- none of these facts alone settles the full historical/canonical identity with the NT Devil.

Status remains `WORKING`, because the requested identity question still requires accountable Second Temple and NT diachronic comparison.

## Unseen Hebrew transfer exam

### Transfer 1 — construct relation
Prompt: "A Hebrew noun is in construct before a divine name. Does that tell us exactly what theological relationship the phrase expresses?"
Answer: No. Construct grammar establishes dependency; lexical range and context determine the relation.
Result: PASS.

### Transfer 2 — article/proper-name shortcut
Prompt: "A supernatural figure has the definite article in one book and lacks it in another. Does that alone prove they are two different beings?"
Answer: No. The distribution is important grammatical/historical evidence, but identity requires narrative, diachronic and comparative argument beyond the article alone.
Result: PASS.

### Transfer 3 — same lemma, ordinary and supernatural uses
Prompt: "Because `שָׂטָן` refers to human adversaries elsewhere, must Job's figure be human?"
Answer: No. Same lemma can realize different referents/senses; Job's heavenly narrative controls the local referent.
Result: PASS.

### Transfer 4 — capitalization
Prompt: "An English Bible capitalizes Spirit/Satan. Does Hebrew capitalization prove the interpretation?"
Answer: No. Biblical Hebrew does not encode the English capitalization distinction; capitalization is an editorial/translation decision informed by interpretation.
Result: PASS.
