# Research Method I — Practicum A

Status: COMPLETE — CANDIDATE_FOR_EXAM
Question: `舊約有聖靈嗎？`

## 1. Initial uncertainty map

The question is underspecified. At least four claims must be separated:
1. Does the Hebrew Bible contain `ruach` language connected with God/YHWH?
2. Does it describe divine activity through God's Spirit?
3. Does the Hebrew Bible itself identify this language with a distinct divine person in the later Christian Trinitarian sense?
4. Can a Christian canonical reading legitimately connect OT Spirit texts with NT pneumatology without pretending that the historical-literary question and the canonical-theological question are identical?

Initial confidence: high for (1–2), unresolved/interpretive for (3–4).

## 2. Evidence plan

Before forming a conclusion:
- inspect primary OT passages using `ruach` + God/YHWH language;
- distinguish senses of `ruach` (wind/breath/spirit) by context;
- sample functions: creation/life, empowerment, prophecy/revelation, renewal/presence;
- consult academically accountable Hebrew Bible scholarship for lexical/historical interpretation;
- actively search for evidence showing that `ruach` is not automatically a reference to the Christian doctrinal category "Holy Spirit";
- keep Christian canonical synthesis in a separate claim layer.

## 3. Evidence sampled

Primary-text anchors for subsequent full-text verification include Gen 1:2; Num 24:2; Judg 3:10; 6:34; 11:29; 14:6; 1 Sam 10:6,10; 16:13–14; Ps 51:11; Isa 11:2; 40:7; 42:1; 61:1; 63:10–11; Ezek 2:2; 11:5; 36:26–27; 37:1,14; Joel 2:28–29.

External scholarly checks located during the practicum:
- TheTorah discussion of Isa 40 explicitly notes the semantic ambiguity `wind/spirit of YHWH` and relates it to Gen 1:2.
- TheTorah study of Balaam (Num 24) identifies `ruach` of God as divine spirit associated with prophetic experience and compares Judges and 1 Samuel.
- A separate Hebrew Bible discussion notes `ruach` language can also denote breath/life, demonstrating that occurrence of the lexeme alone is not sufficient to classify a verse as a Holy-Spirit text.
- The Ahab/Micaiah material supplies a useful counter-check: `ruach` can be modified as a "lying spirit," again proving that `ruach` itself is semantically broader than a technical Trinitarian label.

## 4. Claim/evidence ledger

### Claim A
The OT explicitly uses expressions such as Spirit of God / Spirit of YHWH.
- Evidence: multiple primary-text anchors above.
- Confidence: HIGH.

### Claim B
OT divine-Spirit language is associated with several activities, including creation/life imagery, empowerment, prophecy, divine presence and promised renewal.
- Evidence: distribution across Genesis, Judges/Samuel, Isaiah, Ezekiel, Joel.
- Confidence: HIGH, pending passage-by-passage contextual classification.

### Claim C
Every occurrence of Hebrew `ruach` means the Christian doctrinal person "the Holy Spirit."
- Evidence against: lexical range includes wind, breath, human/other spirit; Isa 40 and other contexts demonstrate ambiguity; 1 Kgs 22 has `ruach sheqer`.
- Decision: REJECT.

### Claim D
The OT by itself presents a fully articulated Nicene/Trinitarian doctrine of the Holy Spirit.
- Evidence: not established merely by divine-Spirit passages.
- Counter-pressure: later doctrinal categories and canonical synthesis must not be silently imported into the historical-literary claim.
- Confidence: LOW as stated; formulation is too strong.

### Claim E
Within Christian canonical theology, OT divine-Spirit passages can be read in relation to NT teaching about the Holy Spirit.
- Claim type: canonical/theological synthesis, not identical to historical lexical observation.
- Confidence: plausible within Christian interpretive framework, but requires a separate canonical practicum before durable consolidation.

## 5. Competing hypotheses

H1 — "There is no Holy Spirit in the OT." Rejected if intended to deny divine-Spirit language/activity: primary texts directly contradict it.

H2 — "The OT already states the later doctrine of the Holy Spirit in full." Not demonstrated by the collected evidence; risks anachronism.

H3 — "The OT contains substantial language and theology of God's Spirit, while the identification and systematic articulation of the Holy Spirit in Christian Trinitarian theology involves later canonical/theological synthesis." Best fit to current evidence.

## 6. Falsification attempt

Potential falsifiers/corrections:
- contexts where `ruach` should be translated wind/breath rather than Spirit;
- textual variants affecting key proof texts;
- scholarship showing a sampled passage has been decontextualized;
- evidence that a supposed divine-Spirit function belongs to a different grammatical subject;
- evidence that the historical claim has been conflated with later canonical theology.

No evidence found that falsifies the narrower observation that the OT repeatedly speaks of God's/YHWH's `ruach` and attributes divine activity to it.

## 7. Bounded conclusion

If `舊約有聖靈嗎？` means "Does the Old Testament speak of God's Spirit and divine activity through the Spirit?" the answer is clearly yes.

If it means "Does every `ruach` occurrence mean the Holy Spirit?" no.

If it means "Does the Old Testament by itself formulate the later Christian doctrine of the Holy Spirit as a distinct person of the Trinity?" that conclusion cannot simply be asserted from lexical occurrence; it requires a separate canonical and theological argument.

Thus Doré should answer by first disambiguating the user's intended level, then distinguish textual observation from Christian theological synthesis.
