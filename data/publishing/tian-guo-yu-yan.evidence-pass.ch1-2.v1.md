## 1. Genesis 27:39 — PROMOTE

The Hebrew oracle to Esau contains a real interpretive ambiguity around `min`: readings divide between a partitive/source sense (`of/from the richness of the earth`) and a separative/privative sense (`away from the richness`). English translations visibly split. The parallel wording with Genesis 27:28 intensifies the problem.

Admission: HIGH. Use as a curiosity opening inside Chapter 1 `聽見`: Isaac's utterance carries irreversible weight while readers/translators millennia later still dispute how the human sentence should be heard. Do not claim the Hebrew itself settles one reading.

## 2. Nimrod plain text vs reception — PROMOTE WITH DISTINCTION

Genesis 10:8–10 presents Nimrod as `gibbōr` / `gibbōr ṣayid`, a mighty figure/hunter, and associates the beginning of his kingdom with Babel, Erech and Akkad in Shinar. The plain notice does not explicitly say he hunts humans, leads the tower project, or that `before YHWH` means `against YHWH`.

Construction consequence: let Nimrod enter first as impressive human power. Later reception can darken him. This better serves Chapter 2 `用力 → 嘆息` than introducing him as a ready-made villain.

## 3. `用口獵人` — PROMOTE, ATTRIBUTION REQUIRED

Rashi on Genesis 10:9, drawing on Genesis Rabbah, says Nimrod ensnared people's minds `with his mouth / by his words` and led them toward rebellion. Bereshit Rabbah develops the ensnaring-through-words line. Ibn Ezra explicitly distinguishes this derash from his own peshat reading of literal animal hunting/offering.

Admission: VERY HIGH. Reader-facing form must say that an old Jewish interpretive tradition makes this move; never say `the Hebrew word hunter means hunting people with speech`.

Waterway legitimately opened:
`蛇的話 → Nimrod mouth/snare → Babel collective speech → language as social power → Jesus' call → Spirit-returned speech`.

## 4. Nimrod name = `marad / rebel` — DEMOTE / CORRECT

The direct etymological equation is too strong. The name's origin is uncertain. The Hebrew `m-r-d` rebellion connection is ancient and powerful as reception/wordplay, but modern scholarship commonly treats it as folk/midrashic etymology rather than demonstrated historical derivation; Mesopotamian proposals including Ninurta exist but are also proposals.

Reconstruction: uncertainty is more interesting than false certainty. Let the `rebel` hearing open reception history and Mesopotamian name questions rather than close them.

## 5. Gilgamesh and Uruk wall — PROMOTE; REMOVE INVENTED SILENCE

The Standard Babylonian epic returns at the end of Tablet XI to Uruk's wall: Gilgamesh tells Urshanabi to inspect the wall, foundation and brickwork. Tablet I likewise opens by directing attention to Uruk's wall. The narrative therefore genuinely circles back to the human-built city after Enkidu's death and the failed immortality quest.

Correction: do not say simply `the epic ends with Gilgamesh silently looking at the wall`. The surviving text gives him speech. Tablet XII is also transmitted after Tablet XI although it does not continue the same narrative sequence cleanly.

Admission: MAJOR. The real structure is stronger: after trying to defeat death, the hero returns to brick, wall, city, inscription and human memory. This crosses naturally with Babel's city/tower/name without identifying Nimrod and Gilgamesh.

## 6. `nacham = sigh` — DEMOTE AS LEXICON, KEEP AS LITERARY INNER ACTION

Genesis 6:6 uses `nacham` in the Niphal and immediately describes God as pained/grieved in his heart. The verb has a broad semantic field involving regret, relenting, grief/comfort and disposition depending on stem/context. Modern lexical scholarship warns against deriving the verse's meaning directly from an alleged concrete etymology such as heavy breathing/sighing.

Rule: do not write `nacham literally means sigh`. Chapter 2's `嘆息` remains valid because grief is already explicit in the scene and because the author uses sigh as a literary/theological inner action. It does not need a lexical trick to survive.

## First evidence-pass verdict

Five structural consequences are now authoritative for reconstruction:

1. Genesis 27:39 becomes a real Chapter 1 curiosity/ambiguity node.
2. `用口獵人` receives正文資格 only with explicit reception-history attribution.
3. Nimrod should first be allowed to feel powerful/impressive before later rebel interpretation closes in.
4. Gilgamesh's actual return to Uruk's wall replaces the invented silent-ending formulation.
5. `Nimrod = rebel` and `nacham = sigh` cease to function as dictionary/etymological facts; both may survive only in accurately labelled reception/literary form.

This evidence pass strengthens rather than weakens the underground structure: uncertainty opens more world than false certainty.
