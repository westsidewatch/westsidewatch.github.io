## Writing constraint added before this pass

**Ban performative transitions.** Do not narrate the author's discovery process to the reader. Avoid default devices such as “等等”, “有意思的是”, “更驚人的是”, “注意”, “你可能沒想到”, or any sentence whose main function is to announce that a connection is clever. Connections must be carried by facts, scenes, chronology, objects, and paragraph sequence. The book's invitation — “這裡有一件很好玩的事，你要不要過來看看？” — is an internal temperament, never a repeated syntactic template.

This pass also rejects AI-like short-sentence stacking, forced suspense, mechanical `不是A而是B`, thesis restatement, and paragraph-end moralizing.

## 1. Uruk is material before it is symbolic

By c. 3200 BCE Uruk was among the largest settlements in the world and already contained monumental mud-brick architecture. Administrative clay tablets recorded goods and worker rations. Later tradition associated a massive city wall with Gilgamesh. This means the move from Nimrod's Erech/Uruk to the Gilgamesh world can proceed historically and materially: city, brick, administration, wall, ruler. It does not need a narrated “discovery” transition.

**Prose admission:** HIGH.

Possible movement:
`示拿 → 以力/Erech → Uruk → mud-brick monumental city → administration/rations → wall → Gilgamesh`.

Do not claim that the biblical Nimrod and Gilgamesh are the same person.

## 2. A city requires organized mouths, hands, and rations

Uruk's early administrative tablets include allocations of barley and other goods. The existence of ration accounting is useful because Babel's collective speech — “來吧” — need not remain an abstraction. Monumental building belongs to a world in which labor has to be gathered, provisioned, counted and coordinated.

**Prose admission:** MEDIUM-HIGH, but avoid inventing a specific Babel work gang or daily ration unless directly evidenced.

Structural value:
`speech → coordination → hands → brick → monument`.

The theological correction remains: fallen human language is not powerless. It can coordinate a city. The question is what reality it serves.

## 3. Mud brick, baked brick, reeds, bitumen

Mesopotamian monumental construction repeatedly combines a mass of mud brick with baked-brick facing and bitumen. Archaeological descriptions from Ur show reed layers, bitumen/matting and mud-brick masses. Babylonian fired bricks survive with bitumen and even impressions of reed matting. British Museum examples preserve bitumen layers up to about 1 cm thick; the museum notes a likely source near Hit on the middle Euphrates, where natural bitumen reaches the surface.

This gives Chapter 2 real matter:
- clay/mud;
- square fired bricks roughly 30+ cm across in Neo-Babylonian examples;
- black bitumen between construction layers;
- reeds/matting leaving physical impressions;
- heat required to turn ordinary clay into durable fired brick.

**Prose admission:** HIGH for material description; chronology must remain explicit. Neo-Babylonian surviving bricks illustrate the long Mesopotamian construction world, not the exact bricks handled by Genesis 11 builders.

Sensory possibilities require discipline. We may describe colour, hardness, dimensions, visible bitumen, reed impressions from objects. Do **not** invent exact smell, kiln temperature, workers' skin, weather, or tactile experience without evidence.

## 4. Genesis 11:3 deserves to remain an action

The biblical sequence gives brickmaking its own line before city/tower construction. This should not be flattened into “they built a tower.” The narrative pauses at the technology of making building material.

Potential inner action:
`沒有石頭 → 人製造可堆疊、可重複、可組織的建築單元 → 用力 becomes scalable`.

The attractive candidate sentence “沒有石頭？那我們就自己造石頭” is **not admitted as factual paraphrase yet**. It is rhetorically effective but risks turning the narrator into a modern explainer and overstates the stone/brick contrast. Keep only as an internal conceptual note until prose testing.

## 5. Names physically enter architecture

Mesopotamian royal building inscriptions make `name` material. A Met brick of Bur-Sin bears a royal inscription commemorating restoration work; such bricks could be buried inside walls for later kings to discover. Neo-Babylonian Nebuchadnezzar II bricks are stamped with royal inscriptions. Foundation cylinders were intentionally buried and baked for survival into the distant future.

This is a first-class Chapter 2 expansion because it does not require us to invent a metaphor. Human names literally entered buildings.

**Prose admission:** VERY HIGH.

Waterway:
`Babel: make a name → Gilgamesh: establish renown → kings stamp names into brick → buildings decay / inscribed objects survive → mortality / memory / sigh`.

Do not say this practice proves the intention of Genesis 11. It is historical material that lets the ancient desire for durable name become visible.

## 6. Gilgamesh: death precedes renown in an early Sumerian composition

ETCSL's Sumerian `Gilgamesh and Huwawa` preserves an unusually strong sequence. Gilgamesh looks over his city, sees people dying and bodies in the river, recognizes that the same end awaits him, and because a human cannot pass beyond life's final limit decides to go to the mountains to establish his renown.

This is stronger than a generic summary “hero wants fame.” The movement is:
`city wall → sees death → knows I too will die → goes out to establish renown`.

It gives the Chapter 2 inner action an evidence-backed dramatic core:
**用力 begins after mortality is seen.**

**Prose admission:** VERY HIGH.

Important textual distinction: this is an early Sumerian Gilgamesh/Huwawa composition, not simply a quotation from the later Standard Babylonian Epic. The book should not merge textual traditions without saying so.

## 7. Cedar expedition: renown has material consequences

In the Sumerian Huwawa composition Gilgamesh travels through mountain ranges and cuts cedar. Some manuscript traditions describe companions piling timber. The expedition therefore joins renown, royal action, scarce material and construction-world imagination.

**Prose admission:** MEDIUM. Valuable if it grows naturally from Gilgamesh; do not overload Chapter 2 with a complete Cedar Forest excursus unless it continues opening the mortality/name line.

## 8. Flood returns inside the Gilgamesh world

The Standard Babylonian Tablet XI has Uta-napishti recount the Deluge. The surviving text speaks to reed fence and brick wall and commands the house to be demolished and a boat built. This is particularly valuable because the material vocabulary — reed, wall, house, building — sits inside a flood survival narrative.

**Prose admission:** HIGH as a later expansion node, but not to manufacture a simplistic “Genesis copied Gilgamesh” argument. The relationship among Mesopotamian flood traditions and Genesis requires separate source-critical treatment.

The present Chapter 2 can let the flood tradition widen the ancient world while postponing theological resolution.

## 9. Babylonian wall rhetoric: building against instability

Nebuchadnezzar II's building cylinder describes a wall built with bitumen and baked brick and presents it as unshakable, with foundations reaching downward and height compared to a mountain. The object itself was baked and buried to survive.

This is useful not because Nebuchadnezzar is a Babel builder, but because it exposes the ancient royal grammar of monumental building: strength, height, permanence, kingly memory.

**Prose admission:** HIGH with chronology explicit.

It can later resonate with the Babel desire for city/tower/name without claiming direct identity.

## 10. The brick survives in a strange way

A Nebuchadnezzar brick in the British Museum is roughly 30+ cm square, stamped, fired, and still carries bitumen and reed-matting impressions. Its royal inscription was originally laid face-down and invisible inside the building. The name was not necessarily a public sign; it could be hidden inside the structure and discovered later.

This refines the earlier line “the name remains on a brick.” The stronger historical point is subtler:
**some names were deliberately put where contemporary eyes would not see them, for a future restoration to find.**

This is a potentially excellent free digression on time, memory and building. It should not be closed with a theological explanation. Let the object remain an object.

## 11. Candidate underground waterway after this pass

The research now supports a longer, less manufactured sequence:

`human sees mortality → seeks renown → city/wall/monument → speech organizes labor → clay becomes standardized brick → royal name enters brick/foundation → structure claims permanence → structure eventually becomes ruin/object/archive → human effort remains impressive and insufficient → sigh`

This is not a new chapter outline. It is an underground waterway that can surface at several points and disappear again.

## 12. First prose-expansion experiment — construction plan only

Do not replace canonical Chapter 2 yet. The first prose experiment should use a **single native opening** and allow a controlled expansion:

1. canonical Nimrod/Erech passage;
2. Erech becomes Uruk without performative transition;
3. material city: mud brick, administration, wall;
4. Gilgamesh appears as a king/tradition of that city;
5. early Sumerian text: he sees death from the wall and seeks renown;
6. cedar expedition may open briefly if rhythm permits;
7. return to name / city / brick;
8. re-enter canonical Babel movement without announcing “now we return to Babel.”

The reader should experience a widening and narrowing of the same world, not a sidebar.

### Explicitly banned in the experiment

- “等等，烏魯克？”
- “有意思的是……”
- “更驚人的是……”
- “這就讓我們想到……”
- “故事到這裡……”
- “你可能已經發現……”
- repeated one-line paragraphs used to simulate drama;
- a moral at the end of every historical paragraph;
- a final paragraph explaining why the digression mattered.

## Result

The material-world pass changes the Chapter 2 expansion from a chain of clever ideas into a chain of **objects and actions**. The strongest new center is not “Nimrod equals Gilgamesh” and not “brick symbolizes pride.” It is the historically grounded human action of trying to make life durable: organizing people, firing clay, raising walls, recording rations, cutting cedar, stamping names, burying inscriptions, and setting out after renown because death has already been seen.

The chapter's inner action remains **用力**. Its emotional result remains **嘆息**. Neither needs to be announced to the reader.
