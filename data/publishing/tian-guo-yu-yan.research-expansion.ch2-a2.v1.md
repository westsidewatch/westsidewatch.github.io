## A2 finding 1 — 「獵人」在古代近東不是小角色

Genesis 10's `gibbōr ṣayid` should be allowed to open into the political imagination of the ancient Near East, but without pretending later Assyrian images are portraits of Nimrod.

Museum evidence gives a strong contextual world:

- royal lion hunting is attested in Mesopotamian ruler imagery before 3000 BCE;
- the lion hunt becomes a durable symbol of kingship, strength, skill, mastery and protection;
- Assyrian ideology later makes the king's defeat of dangerous animals an image of imposing order over chaos;
- lion hunts could be staged royal spectacles and palace images made the ruler visibly heroic.

Reader-facing possibility:

Do not begin by calling Nimrod a villain. Let a reader first understand why an ancient audience could hear `mighty hunter` and imagine ability, danger, kingship and order. Then the question changes: **what happens when the man who can master the wild begins to master people?**

Evidence boundary:

- ADMIT: ancient Near Eastern royal hunt as contextual world.
- DO NOT CLAIM: Assyrian lion-hunt ideology is the direct historical source of Genesis 10.
- DO NOT CLAIM: a specific relief depicts Nimrod.

Primary/authority leads:
- British Museum, `Lion hunting: the sport of kings`.
- Metropolitan Museum, `Animals in Ancient Near Eastern Art`.

## A2 finding 2 — Nimrod's geography itself opens the city-world

Genesis 10:10 places the beginning of Nimrod's kingdom at Babel, Erech/Uruk, Akkad and Calneh in Shinar. Scholarly reconstruction identifies Shinar broadly with southern Mesopotamia / Sumer and Akkad.

This is structurally valuable because the manuscript does not need to jump artificially from Nimrod to Gilgamesh. **Uruk is already named inside Nimrod's biblical geography.** Gilgamesh is the legendary king of Uruk. That does not make Nimrod Gilgamesh; it makes Uruk a legitimate curiosity exit.

Reader path:

`寧錄 → 他的國從哪些城開始？ → Erech 原來就是 Uruk → 那座城留下了另一個古代英雄的故事 → Gilgamesh`

This is a better transition than speculative identity theories.

Evidence boundary:
- ADMIT: Genesis 10 names Erech/Uruk in Nimrod's kingdom list.
- ADMIT: Gilgamesh is legendary king of Uruk.
- BLOCK: `Nimrod = Gilgamesh`.

## A2 finding 3 — Gilgamesh gives Chapter 2 a complete dramatic miniature of 「用力 → 嘆息」

The Met's synthesis provides a usable narrative spine:

`powerful but immature king → Enkidu → friendship → seek a name through heroic exploit → Enkidu dies → Gilgamesh discovers his own mortality → crosses toward Uta-napishtim / Waters of Death → hears flood account → immortality remains unavailable → returns to Uruk → sees the wall / human achievement differently`

The most important discovery for this book is that **making a name is already inside the Gilgamesh story**: Gilgamesh and Enkidu go to the Cedar Forest to make a name for themselves. This can sit beside Babel's explicit `let us make a name for ourselves` without claiming textual dependence.

The second major discovery is architectural: the epic's movement returns to Uruk's defensive wall. Later Mesopotamian inscriptional tradition even remembers the wall as an ancient construction associated with Gilgamesh.

So the Chapter 2 miniature becomes:

`我要留下名字 → 我要完成英雄事業 → 我的朋友死了 → 那我也會死 → 我要去找不死 → 找不到 → 回到城 → 牆還在`

Potential reader-facing line, not yet canonical prose:

**一個走到世界盡頭尋找不死的人，最後回到了磚。**

That line earns further writing because Babel immediately gives us bricks.

Evidence boundary:
- ADMIT: Gilgamesh's immortality quest and return to Uruk wall.
- ADMIT: epic contains a desire to make a name through exploit.
- ADMIT: Tablet XI contains Uta-napishtim's flood account.
- BLOCK/REWRITE: `the epic ends with Gilgamesh silently staring at the wall` unless exact text supports that literary description.

## A2 finding 4 — Flood does not need to be forced into Gilgamesh; it is already there

British Museum K.3375 is the famous Flood Tablet, Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh. Uta-napishtim tells Gilgamesh how the gods sent a flood, he built a boat, took family/creatures aboard, waters subsided and birds were released.

This creates a legitimate Chapter 2 curiosity loop:

`Genesis flood → Nimrod/Uruk → Gilgamesh → quest for immortality → Uta-napishtim → another ancient flood account → back toward the question of mortality`

The flood material must not be used as simplistic proof that one text copied the other. Its literary value here is stranger and better: **Gilgamesh goes looking for immortality and finds a flood survivor.** The man who escaped the destruction still cannot hand ordinary human immortality to Gilgamesh.

This intensifies `嘆息`.

## A2 finding 5 — The city learned to write before the hero could defeat death

Cuneiform emerged in southern Mesopotamian urban contexts before 3200 BCE and was initially used for practical accounting such as bread and beer rations; it then became a writing system used for millennia.

This is a high-value free digression because Chapter 2 is about human civilization becoming astonishingly capable.

Possible movement:

`city → ration → clay → mark → writing → king's name → epic → a dead man's voice survives on clay → yet the man himself still dies`

This can deepen the name/mortality waterway without moralizing it. Humanity discovers how to make words outlive the mouth. That is genuinely magnificent. It still does not solve death.

Evidence boundary:
- ADMIT as historical world.
- Do not make writing itself a Babel punishment/rebellion motif.

## A2 finding 6 — Genesis pauses over brick because brick matters

Genesis 11:3 is unusually material: people exhort one another to make bricks and burn them thoroughly; brick serves for stone and bitumen for mortar. The Hebrew speech intensifies the manufacture (`let us brick bricks` / `burn for burning`).

This deserves scene expansion. Brick is not generic scenery. In the alluvial Mesopotamian plain, monumental construction could rely on mud brick and fired brick rather than abundant quarried stone.

Later Babylon gives extraordinary material confirmation of what this building world could become:

- Etemenanki had an archaeological footprint about 90/91 m square;
- evidence indicates a mud-brick core surrounded by a substantial baked-brick mantle;
- cuneiform/architectural texts describe seven stages and an idealized height around 90 m, though the actual height remains debated;
- Nebuchadnezzar-era building inscriptions repeatedly pair baked brick and bitumen in monumental construction.

Important historical boundary:

Etemenanki is a leading historical/cultural analogue or possible inspiration for the biblical Babel tower tradition, **not an archaeologically proven identification of the Genesis tower**.

Reader-facing scene opportunity:

Before theology, let readers smell bitumen, feel a fired brick, see the flat plain, and understand the technological exhilaration: **we do not have stone; fine, we will make stone.**

That sentence fits Chapter 2's inner action `用力` perfectly.

## A2 finding 7 — The tower is not only height; it is coordinated human power

Genesis 11 repeats collective speech:

`Come, let us make bricks...`

then

`Come, let us build...`

The shared language produces manufacturing, then architecture, then city, then name, then resistance to scattering. God's response explicitly notices `one people / one language` and the scope of what they can plan.

Therefore the language thesis needs refinement:

**fallen human language is not powerless.** It can coordinate labor, build cities, preserve names, command, persuade, boast, deceive and organize enormous social realities. What is broken is not all efficacy; what is broken is truthful alignment with God's creative word.

This is a major bridge from Chapter 1 to Chapter 2.

Potential sequence:

`神說 → 世界成為`

`人說「來吧」 → 磚開始出窯 → 城開始出現`

The resemblance is precisely why the difference matters.

## A2 finding 8 — 「傳揚我們的名」 now has three independent worlds behind it

The name-waterway is stronger than previously mapped:

1. Genesis 11 explicitly says `let us make a name for ourselves`.
2. Gilgamesh and Enkidu undertake heroic action to make a name for themselves.
3. Mesopotamian kings literally stamped/inscribed their names and titles into bricks, cylinders and foundations so royal building memory could survive.

British Museum preserves a Nebuchadnezzar II brick whose stamped inscription names and titles the king. The Met preserves building cylinders designed to survive in foundations and carry royal building memory into the future.

This does **not** prove a direct literary relationship among the three. It gives the book a material world for a universal human impulse:

**if the body dies, perhaps the name can remain in the wall.**

That is Chapter 2's `嘆息` in physical form.

## A2 finding 9 — Etemenanki gives us a startling labor image, but use carefully

A Nebuchadnezzar stele associated with Etemenanki claims mobilization of peoples/rulers from across the world and construction in bitumen and baked brick. UNESCO's Etemenanki dossier also records the later tradition that Alexander's clearance effort involved ten thousand laborers for two months.

These are valuable for scale, but they belong to much later historical Babylon, not the primeval chronology of Genesis 11. They may be used as **what Mesopotamian monumental building eventually looked like**, not as a reconstruction of the biblical workforce.

Potential visual expansion:
- brick molds;
- mud-brick core / fired-brick skin;
- bitumen;
- stamped royal brick;
- ziggurat plan;
- surviving water-filled footprint/pit.

## A2 finding 10 — The most interesting irony may be material, not verbal

The builders fear scattering and want a name that remains.

Babylonian monumental builders later stamp royal names into bricks precisely so names and works survive.

Yet Etemenanki's excellent fired bricks become one reason so much of it disappears: later builders quarry and reuse them. The British Museum notes extensive reuse of Babylon's well-fired bricks; UNESCO likewise notes systematic extraction.

This produces a potentially excellent long-range image:

`we will make bricks → we will make a name → centuries pass → the name survives on displaced bricks while the tower becomes a pit`

No moral needs to be appended. The image can do the work.
